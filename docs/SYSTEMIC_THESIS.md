## 1. The Executive Thesis: A Crisis of Unit Economics and Structural Obsolescence

The United States credit and collections ecosystem stands at a precipice of functional obsolescence, driven by a fundamental architectural mismatch between the legacy infrastructure of debt recovery and the modern reality of consumer liability.

**Core Thesis:** The prevailing U.S. credit and collections system was engineered for a macroeconomic environment that no longer exists--one defined by macro-debts such as 30-year fixed-rate mortgages, automotive loans, and consolidated revolving credit lines. It was never designed, and remains structurally ill-equipped, to manage the explosion of small-balance, high-velocity consumer debts generated by Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) platforms, gig-economy services, subscription models, and micro-credit products.

This incompatibility is not merely a friction point; it is a **systemic failure of unit economics**. The cost to recover a marginal dollar of debt using traditional human-centric methods now frequently exceeds the value of the dollar itself.

### The Atomization Problem

We are witnessing the "atomization" of consumer debt, where a single individual's financial liabilities are fragmented across dozens of unregulated or semi-regulated platforms rather than concentrated in a few major banking relationships. This fragmentation destroys the economies of scale that traditional collection agencies rely upon, rendering the standard operating procedure--the human agent call center--**economically insolvent** for a vast and growing segment of the credit market.

### Systemic Implications

1. **Sub-Prime Tax**: Vulnerable consumers face inflated costs for essential financial services to subsidize recovery system inefficiency
2. **Regulatory Friction**: Compliance acts as a barrier to resolution rather than a safeguard
3. **Market Failure**: $400B+ in recoverable value abandoned annually due to negative unit economics

**Required Response:** A radical paradigm shift toward autonomous, "Agentic AI" driven recovery models and the securitization of micro-debts via blockchain-based Real-World Asset (RWA) tokenization to restore liquidity and operational viability to the consumer credit market.

## 2. The Historical and Structural Divergence of Debt Architectures

To understand the magnitude of the current crisis, one must analyze the genealogy of the U.S. collections infrastructure. The system is operating on a chassis built in the mid-20th century, optimized for a completely different set of financial physics.

### 2.1 The Legacy Paradigm: Macro-Debt and Human Intervention

Post-World War II American consumer credit was characterized by "lumpy" debt. A typical household might have a mortgage, a car loan, and perhaps a department store credit line. These debts were substantial in principal, originated by regulated depository institutions, and underwritten with significant friction (paper applications, manual review).

In this environment, the collections model was straightforward: human intervention. When a loan of $15,000 went into default, deploying a human agent to spend hours skip-tracing, calling, and negotiating was a rational allocation of resources. The "Cost to Collect" (CTC) was a fraction of the "Value at Risk" (VaR).

The infrastructure that calcified around this model--large call centers, predictive dialers, and manual legal processing--was designed to amortize high fixed costs over high-value recovery targets.

**Regulatory Framework (FDCPA, 1977):**
The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), enacted in 1977, assumed a world where communication was synchronous and intrusive (the telephone). It built guardrails around harassment but did not anticipate a world where a consumer might have 20 concurrent distinct debt obligations, each requiring separate compliant communications.

### 2.2 The Modern Paradigm: The Atomization of Liability

The financialization of the digital economy has shattered the "lumpy" debt model. We have moved to a "granular" debt model defined by three key vectors:

| Feature | Legacy Debt Model (1970-2010) | Modern Micro-Debt Model (2020-Present) |
|---------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Primary Debt Vehicles | Mortgages, Auto Loans, Credit Cards | BNPL, Subscriptions, Gig-Advances, Micro-loans |
| Average Balance | High ($5k - $300k) | Low ($20 - $500) |
| Underwriting Speed | Days/Weeks | Milliseconds/Seconds |
| Origination Source | Banks, Credit Unions | Fintechs, Merchants, Apps |
| Data Visibility | High (Centralized Credit Bureaus) | Low (Fragmented/Phantom Debt) |
| Recovery Method | Human Agents, Litigation | Digital Nudges, AI Agents, Write-offs |

**Table 1: The Structural Divergence of Consumer Credit Architectures.** This table illustrates the fundamental mismatch. The US collections system is trying to use "Legacy Debt Model" tools to solve "Modern Micro-Debt Model" problems. The result is a system that is too slow, too expensive, and too legally perilous to function effectively.

**Example Consumer Debt Profile (2026):**
- 5 active BNPL loans (Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay, Sezzle, Zip)
- 3 subscription arrears (Netflix, Spotify, gym)
- 2 gig-economy advances (Uber, DoorDash)
- 1 telecom equipment balance
- 1 medical copay
- 1 overdraft fee

**Total obligations:** 13 separate creditors | **Average balance:** $87 | **Traditional CTC per account:** $47 | **Economic outcome:** Uncollectible by definition

## 3. The Unit Economics of Friction: Anatomy of a Broken P&L

The central failure point of the current system is mathematical. The operational cost of compliant human-centric collection has effectively imposed a "floor" on the size of debt that is economically recoverable. As the average debt size falls below this floor, the system ceases to function, leading to massive inefficiencies and consumer harm.

### 3.1 The Insolvency of the Human Agent Model

The "Agent-Based" collections model is predicated on the efficiency of human labor. However, labor costs in the US have risen, while the efficiency of reaching consumers via phone has plummeted due to call blocking, spam labeling, and changing consumer behavior.

**The Cost of Conversation:** Benchmarking data for 2024-2025 indicates that the fully burdened cost of a human agent in a specialized financial services role ranges between **$3.00 and $6.50 per minute** of active handle time. This figure includes:

- **Direct Compensation:** Wages, commissions, and bonuses.
- **Benefits & Taxes:** Healthcare, insurance, payroll taxes (often 30% of base pay).
- **Infrastructure:** Seat licenses for dialers, CRM software, compliance tools, and physical or remote workspace costs.
- **Management Overhead:** QA monitoring, team leads, training, and HR support.

**The Micro-Debt Recovery Trap:**

Consider a typical BNPL default of $50.

**Scenario A (Human Agent):** An agent spends 2 minutes reviewing the file and 5 minutes attempting contact (leaving voicemails, navigating IVRs). If they connect, they spend 8 minutes negotiating a payment plan.
- Total Time: 15 minutes
- Operational Cost: 15 mins x $4.00/min = **$60.00**
- Result: The agency has spent $60 to recover $50. This is a **guaranteed loss**.

Even if the agent is highly efficient and closes the deal in 5 minutes, the cost ($20) represents 40% of the principal. When factoring in the "contact rate"--the reality that an agent might make 20 calls to get one "Right Party Contact" (RPC)--the economics disintegrate further. A 5% RPC rate means 19 calls are wasted cost.

For micro-debts, the "Cost to Collect" (CTC) often exceeds 100% of the recovery value. This forces agencies to abandon small balances, creating a **"collection void."** Debts under a certain threshold (often $100-$200) are simply written off or warehoused, because "working" them is financially irrational.

### 3.2 The Sub-Prime Tax and the Cost of Inefficiency

The inefficiency of the collections system is not absorbed by the lenders; it is passed on to the consumer in the form of higher interest rates and fees. This phenomenon is quantifiable as the **"Sub-Prime Tax."**

Because lenders know that recovering small defaulted amounts is cost-prohibitive, they must price their products to account for a higher loss severity. A borrower with a 620 credit score pays an average **"tax" of nearly $3,400 per year** in excess interest and insurance premiums compared to a prime borrower.

- **Auto Loans:** Subprime borrowers pay ~$745 more per year in interest.
- **Insurance:** Credit-based insurance scoring means subprime drivers pay ~$514 more annually.
- **Mortgages:** The disparity in mortgage interest can amount to over $1,300 annually.

This tax is a direct result of the system's inability to efficiently recover losses. If the collections system were frictionless and cheap (i.e., if recovering $50 cost $0.50), lenders could lower the risk premium charged to borrowers. **The structural inefficiency of the legacy collections model effectively extracts wealth from the poorest consumers to pay for the obsolescence of the recovery infrastructure.**

### 3.3 The BNPL Delinquency Paradox

Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) has exacerbated this crisis by lowering the barrier to entry for credit while increasing the complexity of repayment.

- **Rising Delinquency:** In 2025, delinquency rates for BNPL users spiked, with **41% of users reporting a late payment**. This is significantly higher than traditional credit card delinquency rates.
- **Loan Stacking:** The average BNPL user is not replacing credit cards but stacking BNPL loans on top of existing debt. **63% of BNPL borrowers have multiple active loans simultaneously.** This creates a liquidity crunch where the consumer has fragmented their disposable income across too many creditors.
- **The Race to Liquidity:** In a "stacked" scenario, the creditor who gets paid is the one with the least friction. A human agent calling a millennial consumer is high friction. An automated deduction or a seamless one-click payment text is low friction. The legacy system loses this race every time.

### 3.4 The Break-Even Calculation

Traditional collections operates on a cost structure:

```
CTC_traditional = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs
                = (Skip Trace + Compliance Setup + Agent Training)

```

For a debt to be economically recoverable:

```
Expected Recovery Value > CTC
Balance x Recovery Probability > $47
```

## 4. The Regulatory Moat: Compliance as a Barrier to Innovation

While technology offers potential solutions to the unit economics crisis, the regulatory framework acts as a powerful brake on innovation. The rules governing debt collection were written for a different technological era and often criminalize the very efficiency that is needed to solve the micro-debt problem.

### 4.1 Regulation F and the Frequency Cap

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's (CFPB) Regulation F, fully implemented in late 2021, introduced the **"7/7/7" rule**: a debt collector is presumed to be harassing a consumer if they place a telephone call more than seven times within a seven-day period or within seven days of engaging in a telephone conversation.

- **Impact on Micro-Debt Strategy:** For large debts, a "7 calls in 7 days" limit is a reasonable constraint; the lifecycle of a mortgage default is measured in months. For a micro-debt like a $30 BNPL installment, which may be tied to a bi-weekly paycheck cycle, speed is essential.
- **Throughput Constraint:** The 7/7/7 rule effectively caps the velocity of collection. If an agency cannot reach a consumer in the first week, they are legally throttled. For high-volume, low-balance portfolios, this throttle destroys the ability to "work" the volume necessary to find paying consumers.
- **Channel Ambiguity:** While the rule explicitly counts phone calls, the status of newer channels (SMS, email, push notifications) remains complex. Regulation F allows for unlimited emails and texts provided the consumer has a reasonable way to opt-out, but the "Limited Content Message" rules for voicemail create a minefield.

### 4.2 The "Mini-Miranda" and AI Liability

The deployment of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to lower costs is complicated by strict disclosure requirements. The FDCPA mandates the "Mini-Miranda" warning ("This is a communication from a debt collector...") in the initial communication and subsequent interactions.

**The "Hallucination" Risk:** Generative AI models (LLMs) are probabilistic, not deterministic. There is a non-zero risk that an AI agent, in an effort to be conversational, might:

- **Omit the disclosure:** Failing to state the Mini-Miranda is a strict liability offense.
- **Make false threats:** An AI might "hallucinate" a legal threat ("We will garnish your wages") that the creditor has not authorized. Under the FDCPA, threatening action that cannot or is not intended to be taken is illegal.
- **Impersonate Authority:** An AI must clearly disclose that it is a machine. If it simulates a human so well that the consumer is deceived, this creates "Unfair, Deceptive, or Abusive Acts or Practices" (UDAAP) liability.

This creates a **"Compliance-by-Design" mandate**. AI systems cannot simply be "trained" on data; they must have hard-coded "guardrails" or deterministic logic layers that prevent non-compliant output regardless of the conversation flow.

### 4.3 The Fragmentation of State Sovereignty

The U.S. does not have a single debt collection market; it has 50. States like New York, California, and Massachusetts have enacted regulations that far exceed federal standards.

- **New York:** Requires strict substantiation of the debt before collection can begin and has reduced the statute of limitations for consumer debts.
- **California:** The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and subsequent revisions grant consumers immense power over their data, including the right to demand deletion, which conflicts with the record-keeping requirements of the FDCPA.
- **State Licensing:** Agencies must be licensed in every state they collect in. For a fintech startup lending nationally, managing 50 different compliance regimes for $50 debts is an administrative nightmare that favors incumbent giants and stifles competition.

**Regulatory Friction Points:**

| Regulation | Original Intent | Friction in Micro-Debt Context |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------------------|
| FDCPA (1977) | Prevent harassment via phone | Criminalizes efficient digital contact strategies needed for high-volume accounts |
| Reg F (7/7/7 Rule) | Limit call frequency | Caps throughput for short-term debts (BNPL) where velocity is critical |
| TCPA (1991) | Stop robocalls | Creates liability for AI-driven SMS/Voice outreach without express consent |
| Mini-Miranda | Disclose collector identity | "Hallucination" risk for AI; strict liability trap for automated systems |
| State Licensing | Local oversight | Administrative nightmare for national fintech apps; massive overhead per small debt |

**Table 3: Regulatory Friction Points for Modern Debt**

## 5. The Data Infrastructure Void: Metro 2 and the "Credit Invisible"

The "connective tissue" of the credit system--the data that flows between lenders, bureaus, and collectors--is fundamentally broken. The industry standard format, Metro 2, is a relic of the mainframe era, incapable of handling the nuance and velocity of modern micro-credit.

### 5.1 The Metro 2 Bottleneck

Metro 2 is a batch-processing format designed for monthly reporting. It describes debts in rigid fields: "Account Status," "Balance," "Date of Last Payment." It lacks the flexibility to describe the complex, fluid nature of gig-economy finance.

- **Latency:** A BNPL loan (often 4 payments over 6 weeks) moves faster than the Metro 2 reporting cycle. A consumer could open a loan, default, and cure the default before the monthly Metro 2 file is even processed by the bureau. This means credit reports are perpetually out of sync with reality.
- **Categorization Failure:** Metro 2 does not have a native "BNPL" trade line code. Bureaus have to shoehorn these debts into "unsecured installment loan" or "revolving line" categories. This misclassification can harm consumers; for example, opening a small BNPL loan might lower the "average age of accounts" on a credit file, dropping the score, even if the loan is paid perfectly.
- **Furnishing Friction:** The technical barrier to producing compliant Metro 2 files is high. Small fintechs and subscription services often choose not to report at all because the cost of handling disputes (which requires e-OSCAR integration and dedicated staff) outweighs the benefit.

### 5.2 The National Consumer Assistance Plan (NCAP) and Data Suppression

The National Consumer Assistance Plan (NCAP), born from a settlement with state attorneys general, has systematically scrubbed "negative" data from credit reports to protect consumers. While well-intentioned, this has created a data void.

- **Medical Debt Scrub:** As of 2023-2024, paid medical collection debt and medical collections under $500 are no longer reported.
- **Public Records:** Civil judgments and tax liens have been largely removed due to stricter matching standards.

**The "Blind" Lender:** A lender underwriting a new loan today cannot see if the applicant has defaulted on five $400 medical bills or has a $5,000 civil judgment against them. This "data suppression" forces lenders to assume higher risk for everyone, raising prices (the sub-prime tax) and pushing them to rely on invasive "alternative data."

### 5.3 The Rise of Alternative Data and "Trigger Leads"

To fill the void left by NCAP and the Metro 2 lag, the industry is turning to "alternative data": scanning bank account cash flows, scraping utility payment history, and monitoring rental payments.

- **Privacy vs. Access:** While this data can help "credit invisible" consumers get approved, it effectively ends financial privacy. To get a micro-loan, a consumer must grant an API (like Plaid) deep access to their entire banking history.
- **Trigger Leads:** The monetization of this data has become aggressive. When a lender pulls a credit report (a "hard inquiry"), the bureaus sell that fact as a "trigger lead" to other lenders, who immediately bombard the consumer with competing offers. This commoditization of the consumer's intent is a direct revenue stream for bureaus compensating for the low margins of basic reporting.

## 6. Behavioral Economics: The Psychology of Micro-Debt

The failure of the legacy model is also psychological. The collections system assumes a "Rational Economic Man" who prioritizes debts based on interest rates and legal consequences. The modern consumer, overwhelmed by micro-transactions, behaves very differently.

### 6.1 Rational Inattention and the "Ostrich Effect"

Faced with the cognitive load of managing dozens of subscriptions, BNPL payments, and bills, consumers engage in **"Rational Inattention."** The cost (in time and stress) of processing the information exceeds the benefit of resolving the $20 debt. They simply stop looking.

- **The Ostrich Effect:** Consumers actively avoid financial information when they are stressed. Traditional collections--which rely on increasing the frequency and urgency of contact--actually worsen this effect, causing the consumer to bury their head deeper in the sand.
- **Information Overload:** In an attention economy, a debt collection email competes with hundreds of marketing emails. It is easily filtered out. The "signal" of the debt is lost in the "noise" of digital life.

### 6.2 The "Snowball" Preference

Behavioral research contradicts the "efficient" repayment strategy (paying highest interest first). Consumers overwhelmingly prefer the **Snowball Method**: paying off the smallest balances first to get a psychological "win" and reduce the sheer number of open obligations.

**Implication for Collections:** Strategies that aggregate debt or offer "lump sum" settlements for multiple small items are more psychologically attractive than trying to collect each line item individually. The consumer wants to reduce complexity, not just liability.

### 6.3 Gamification as Engagement

To combat the "Ostrich Effect," progressive models are moving from "collection" to "engagement" using gamification.

- **Case Study: Wandoo Finance:** This lender replaced traditional threatening reminders with an interactive, gamified interface. Users earned "points" or status for repayment behaviors. This approach, tapping into the dopamine loops of mobile gaming, significantly increased repayment rates compared to traditional methods.
- **Symend's "Behavioral Engagement":** Symend utilizes behavioral science to tailor communications. Instead of "You owe $50," the message might be "Hi [Name], we know life gets busy. Here is a flexible way to get back on track." By reducing the "shame" and "pain" of paying, they unlock payments from consumers who are willing but stressed.

## 7. The Technological Remediation: Agentic AI and the Compute-Centric Model

The only viable path to resolving the unit economics crisis is the complete removal of human labor from the micro-debt recovery chain. This is not automation in the sense of "robocalls"; it is the deployment of autonomous, cognitive **"Agentic AI."**

### 7.1 The Economics of Agentic AI

"Agentic AI" refers to systems driven by Large Language Models (LLMs) that can reason, use tools, and negotiate autonomously.

- **Cost Arbitrage:** While a human agent costs ~$4.00/minute, an AI voice agent (using services like Retell AI or Vapi) costs between **$0.08 and $0.20 per minute**. This 95%+ reduction in marginal cost makes it economically viable to have a "high-touch" negotiation for a $30 debt.
- **Scale:** An AI system can scale from 100 concurrent calls to 100,000 instantly. This elasticity is crucial for BNPL lenders who might see a spike in defaults after a holiday shopping season.

|--------|-----------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Cost Per Minute | $3.00 - $6.50 | $0.50 - $1.00 | $0.08 - $0.20 | < $0.01 |
| Setup Cost | High (Hiring/Training) | Medium (Vendor Mgmt) | Low (API Integration) | Low (SaaS) |
| Scalability | Low (Weeks/Months) | Medium (Days/Weeks) | Instant | Instant |
| Compliance Risk | High (Human Error) | High (Script Adherence) | Low (Code-Constrained) | Low (Template Based) |
| Min. Viable Debt | $200 - $300 | $50 - $100 | $5 | $1 |

**Table 2: Comparative Unit Economics of Recovery Channels**

### 7.2 The Architecture of Autonomy

The modern AI collections stack is a sophisticated integration of multiple technologies:

- **Orchestrator LLM:** The "brain" (e.g., GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet) that understands the conversation context and decides on the strategy.
- **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation):** The system pulls the specific debtor's details (balance, payment history) and the specific compliance rules (state laws) from a vector database in real-time to ground the conversation in fact.
- **Voice Synthesis (TTS/STT):** Low-latency (sub-800ms) voice conversion that makes the AI sound indistinguishable from a human, crucial for maintaining engagement.
- **Compliance Monitor:** A separate, "Constitutional AI" model that listens to the conversation in real-time and acts as a kill-switch if the primary agent begins to hallucinate or violate the FDCPA.

### 7.3 Vertical vs. Horizontal AI

Generic AI models (Horizontal AI) like ChatGPT are insufficient for collections because they lack domain expertise and risk awareness. The industry is moving toward **Vertical AI**--models fine-tuned specifically on millions of debt collection conversations. These models understand the nuance of "I can't pay until Friday" vs. "I refuse to pay," and can navigate the negotiation "dance" more effectively than a generic model.

## 8. The Financial Remediation: Tokenization and DeFi Liquidity

While AI solves the recovery problem, it does not solve the liquidity problem for lenders holding millions of dollars in non-performing micro-loans. The solution here lies in the securitization of these debts using blockchain technology.

### 8.1 The Illiquidity of NPL Portfolios

Currently, selling a portfolio of charged-off BNPL debt is a slow, opaque process. Lenders package debts into spreadsheets and sell them to debt buyers for pennies on the dollar. The "bid-ask spread" is massive because buyers don't trust the data quality and can't easily verify the assets.

### 8.2 Real-World Asset (RWA) Tokenization

Protocols like Centrifuge are revolutionizing this by moving the securitization process on-chain.

- **NFT Representation:** Each debt (or batch of debts) is minted as a Non-Fungible Token (NFT) on a blockchain. This NFT contains immutable metadata about the loan (origination, payment history, risk score).
- **Transparency:** Because the data is on-chain, a potential buyer can audit the entire portfolio's performance in real-time, eliminating the "lemon market" problem of traditional debt sales.

### 8.3 The Tinlake Tranche Model

The Centrifuge/Tinlake protocol structures these pools into risk tranches, similar to traditional securitization but programmed into smart contracts:

- **DROP Token (Senior Tranche):** Investors in this tranche get paid first. They accept a lower yield (e.g., 5-8%) for safety. This is protected against the first wave of defaults.
- **TIN Token (Junior Tranche):** Investors here take the "first loss" position. They only get paid after the DROP holders are satisfied. In exchange, they get the upside yield (e.g., 12-20%).
- **Algorithmic Waterfall:** The smart contract automatically routes repayments to the correct tranche. There is no "servicer" taking a fee or delaying payment. The code is the servicer.

**The Tokenized Debt Stack:**

| Layer | Function | Technology/Mechanism |
|-------|----------|---------------------|
| Asset Originator | Originates the loan (BNPL, Invoice) | Fintech App / Lender |
| Tokenization | Mints NFT representing the asset/collateral | Centrifuge P2P Protocol |
| Pooling | Aggregates NFTs into a smart contract pool | Tinlake / Centrifuge Chain |
| Tranching | Splits risk into Senior (DROP) and Junior (TIN) | Smart Contract Logic |
| Liquidity | Provides capital (Stablecoins/DAI) against tokens | DeFi Protocols (MakerDAO, Aave) |
| Servicing | Collects payments and distributes to tranches | Automated Waterfall Contract |

**Table 4: The Tokenized Debt Stack (Centrifuge/Tinlake Model)**

### 8.4 Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Integration

This infrastructure allows BNPL lenders to access global liquidity from the DeFi market. A lender can originate loans, tokenize them, and pledge them as collateral to a decentralized protocol (like MakerDAO or Aave) to borrow stablecoins (USDC) instantly. This "capital velocity" allows them to recycle funds much faster than waiting for a traditional bank warehouse facility.

The convergence of these forces--the collapse of unit economics, the rigor of regulation, the opacity of data, and the emergence of AI and Blockchain--points to a singular conclusion: **The legacy model of debt collection is dead.** It is not merely inefficient; it is structurally incapable of functioning in the modern economy.

### The New Model

- **Fully Autonomous Recovery:** For debts under $1,000, human intervention will be eliminated. Agentic AI will handle 100% of the lifecycle, from digital nudges to voice negotiation, at a cost of cents per dollar.
- **Programmatic Compliance:** Regulation will be codified into the software stack. "Compliance" will not be a department but a software library that constrains AI behavior in real-time.
- **On-Chain Liquidity:** The secondary market for debt will migrate to blockchain rails. Debt will be tokenized at origination, allowing for fluid, transparent trading and instant liquidity for lenders.
- **Behavioral-First Engagement:** The adversarial "collections" approach will be replaced by a "financial wellness" approach, where the goal is to rehabilitate the consumer's LTV (Lifetime Value) rather than just extracting a one-time payment.

The entities that cling to the call center model and Metro 2 batches will find themselves on the wrong side of history, burdened by unrecoverable costs and regulatory fines. The future belongs to those who treat debt not as a moral failing to be punished, but as a data problem to be solved with compute, code, and cryptography.
